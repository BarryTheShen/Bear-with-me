Verdict

I checked their whole catalogue with the store set to Brentwood, not just two SKUs. They stock readers, writers and even Flipper Zero — but no NTAG. Every writable tag on the shelf is MIFARE Classic, which an iPhone cannot read at all and which Android supports only optionally. Why this is survivable

The tag was only ever a way to carry a URL. A printed QR carries the same URL, is read by every phone's native camera with no app, and costs nothing. Your relay, your privacy model, your live owner notification and your whole evidence story are unchanged.

You lose one thing: the invisible-until-tapped aesthetic. Keep that as the stated production form factor and show the datasheet numbers — a 20 × 10 × 0.2 mm inlay — on a slide. Judges accept a clearly-reasoned roadmap. They do not accept a demo that does not run.

1. Micro Center Brentwood: everything they actually have

You asked whether Micro Center has any NFC chip at all. They have five, and I checked each product page with the store set to Brentwood rather than trusting the default catalogue view — that distinction matters, because the store-less page reports Not Available on items that are physically on the shelf. Verified today:

The MIFARE Classic problem, and how far it actually sinks you

Every writable tag Micro Center has is MIFARE Classic S50 — the bracelet, and the card and fob in the RC522 kit. Keyestudio's own spec sheet lists the supported types as mifare1 S50, mifare1 S70.

1. An iPhone cannot read it. At all. Apple's Core NFC exposes a MIFARE family of exactly four values — desfire, plus, ultralight and unknown. Classic is not in the list and is not mentioned on the page. The no-app iPhone tap is precisely what this chip cannot do.

2. Not every Android can read it either. This is the part that will bite you if you skip it. Google's own documentation says support for MIFARE Classic is optional on Android: “Implementation of this class on a Android NFC device is optional. If it is not implemented, then MifareClassic will never be enumerated.” In practice it tracks the NFC chipset — NXP-based phones generally do, others generally do not.

So test it in the car park before you build anything on it. Install NFC Tools on the demo phone, tap the card, and look for MIFARE Classic in the tech list. Thirty seconds, and it tells you whether you have an NFC demo or a QR demo.

Source: Apple, NFCMiFareFamily — Core NFC (2026); Google, MifareClassic — Android NFC reference (2026); Keyestudio, KS0067 keyestudio RC522 RFID Module for Arduino (2026); Micro Center, Adafruit MIFARE Classic 13.56MHz RFID/NFC 1KB bracelet, SKU 471797 (2026); Micro Center, Inland KS0067 RC522 RFID module, SKU 503508 (2026).
What to actually buy: the $6.99 kit, not the $3.99 bracelet

Same wrong chip, but the RC522 kit is strictly the better $7: two tags instead of one (card plus fob, so you can tag two demo objects), plus a reader you can use later. And per NXP's datasheet the MFRC522 “supports ISO/IEC 14443 A/MIFARE and NTAG” — so if a real NTAG turns up from any of the phone calls, this same board can write it.

Buy both if you want three tagged objects. It is $11 total. Just never let MIFARE Classic be your only path to a working demo.

Source: NXP Semiconductors, MFRC522 standard performance MIFARE and NTAG frontend, data sheet (2016); Micro Center, Inland KS0067 RC522 RFID module, SKU 503508 (2026); Micro Center, Adafruit MIFARE Classic 13.56MHz RFID/NFC 1KB bracelet, SKU 471797 (2026).
Do not buy the PN532 or the Flipper for this

Both are readers/writers. Neither ships a tag, so neither solves your problem — you would be spending $40 or $200 to write to tags you do not have. The PN532 can in principle emulate a tag for a phone to read, but that is an unbudgeted evening of firmware work on a board you would then have to hide inside a demo object, and I have not verified an iPhone reads it. With 30 hours on the clock that is a gamble, not a plan.

4. The QR build, and what I already changed

Three things are already added and tested in your app
  • /labels — a printable sheet of QR labels, one per tag, each carrying a 22 mm QR plus the human-readable code. Hit print. Tested: renders the codes and QRs and includes a print button.
  • /f — a typed-code fallback. A finder enters six characters and lands on the same finder page. Works with no NFC, no camera and no app. Tested, including lowercase input.
  • /write/CODE — writes an NFC tag straight from Chrome on Android using the Web NFC API, so if a tag does turn up you need no writing app at all.
Print size is the one thing that will bite you

DENSO's specification requires a 4-module quiet zone on all four sides. A version-1 QR is 21 modules plus 8 of quiet zone, so at 0.5 mm per module the whole symbol is 14.5 mm. That is the mathematical floor, not a guarantee on a phone camera.

Print at 22–30 mm square, black on white, no logo in the middle. Keep the URL short so the code stays at version 1 or 2. The label sheet already does this — just do not let the print dialog scale it down.

Source: DENSO WAVE, QR code area and quiet zone (2026); DENSO WAVE, QR module size guidance (2026).

5. If a tag does turn up

Switch to it immediately for the demo item, and keep QR on the rest. Two facts make this fast:

  • You do not need a writing app. Chrome for Android can write NDEF tags from a web page via NDEFReader.write(), over HTTPS, with a permission prompt. Open /write/CODE on an Android phone, tap the button, hold the tag. Done.
  • Check the chip before you rely on it. It must be NTAG213/215/216. A MIFARE Classic card — which is what most Arduino RFID kits ship with — is not reliably readable by iPhone, and will not trigger the no-app notification.
  • A bare card UID will not work for the magic demo. Apple's background reading is NDEF-only. Reading a raw card identifier needs an installed app using NFCTagReaderSession. If all you can find is an old access fob, you have lost the no-app tap, which is the whole point. Stay on QR.

Source: MDN, NDEFReader: write() method (2026) ✓; Google Chrome, Interact with NFC devices on the web (2026); Apple, Core NFC overview (2026).

6. How to pitch it without the NFC

Turn the constraint into the argument

Do not apologise for the QR. Use it to make the point that the identity carrier is interchangeable and the system is the product:

“The code on this bottle is a QR because we built it this weekend. In production it is a 20 by 10 millimetre NFC inlay, two tenths of a millimetre thick, hidden under the label — you tap it and it just works, no app. Either way the interesting part is the same: the finder never learns who you are, the owner is told in real time, and the university already knows how to reach every student.”

Then hit them with the evidence: phones are returned at 82.8% and umbrellas at 1.4%, because phones can say whose they are. And microchipped stray cats are returned at 38.5% against 1.8% for strays generally. That is your thesis, and it does not depend on which carrier you used this weekend.

One more thing worth saying out loud, because it is genuinely your strongest campus insight: the most commonly lost item on a university campus is a water bottle, named first by Toronto, McGill, Whitman, Indiana and UT Austin — and Exeter and UNSW throw water bottles away on arrival because storing them is not worth the shelf space. The single most-lost object on campus is the one lost-property offices bin immediately. Fixing that needs a two-cent label, not a chip.
