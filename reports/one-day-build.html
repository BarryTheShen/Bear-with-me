1. How big is it, exactly

You conflated two products. They are different, and the difference matters.

What that actually feels like

The recommended tag is 20 × 10 mm and 0.2 mm thick. So:

  • Footprint: smaller than an adult thumbnail. Shorter than a US penny is wide (19.05 mm) in one direction, half that in the other.
  • Thickness: about two sheets of office paper. A US penny is 1.52 mm, so the tag is roughly eight times thinner than a penny.
  • Feel: flexible, and under a printed label you cannot feel a step with a fingertip.

The Avery round one is thinner still — 0.136 mm, around 1.4 sheets of paper, or roughly two human hairs — but it is 22 mm across.

Why you cannot just go smaller

Range comes from the area of the antenna coil, because this is near-field magnetic coupling. Shrinking the tag shrinks the coil and the range collapses fast. A 6 mm round tag is sold, and its vendor quotes a read range of about 2 mm — that is not a tap, that is touching it in exactly the right spot.

Peer-reviewed phone measurements: a 160 × 80 mm coil read at 8.1 cm, an 80 × 80 mm coil at 5.0 cm, and the best commercial tag tested at 5.6 cm. At 20 × 10 mm expect roughly 1–2 cm — fine for a deliberate tap, useless for a wave.

2. Shopping list

Two purchases that will waste your day
  • Generic ‘NFC stickers’ with no chip named. If the listing does not say NTAG213, NTAG215 or NTAG216, do not buy it.
  • MIFARE Classic tags. Widely sold, cheap, and not reliably readable by iPhone. SparkFun's adhesive RFID tag is one of these.

3. The hour-by-hour plan

The software is done

research/prototype/app.py is a complete, tested implementation: create tags, owner claims a tag, finder taps and reports a location, owner's dashboard updates live. It is pure Python standard library — no pip install, no API keys, no accounts.

The whole loop was tested end to end: a finder submission reached the owner's dashboard in real time, and the finder page names the item without revealing anything about the owner.

Run it: python3 app.py, then ngrok http 8000 and restart with BASE_URL=https://your.ngrok.app python3 app.py.

4. Writing the tags, with no code

Use NFC Tools or NXP TagWriter. Both are free. The NXP manual gives the exact sequence:

  1. Copy the tag URL from the claim page — it looks like https://your.ngrok.app/i/MXFNV8.
  2. Open NFC TagWriter → Write tags → New dataset → LINK.
  3. Paste the URL → SAVE & WRITE → WRITE, then hold the phone against the tag until it confirms.
  4. For a batch: Write tags → My Datasets → Extras → Write Multiple.
  5. Test by tapping with a different phone. Only then consider Protect tags → Lock tag — this is permanent and irreversible, so keep spare unlocked tags.

5. Sticking it on, and the metal problem

Read this before you glue anything

A plain NFC tag on steel or aluminium does not work. The metal develops eddy currents that oppose the reader's field and detune the tag. It does not degrade gracefully — it just fails, silently, in front of the judge.

Your steel water bottle and your aluminium laptop lid both need a ferrite-backed on-metal tag, which is about 38 mm and opaque. If you cannot get one today, stick the tag on the plastic underside of the laptop, or on a plastic sleeve on the bottle, or hold it off the metal with a 3–5 mm plastic spacer. Do not improvise this on stage.

Source: NXP Semiconductors, AN13219 — NFC antenna design with ferrite (2021); Avery Dennison, AD Bullseye On-Metal data sheet (2026).

7. The demo script

Give the judge the phone

Do not demo it yourself. Hand the judge their own phone situation: put the tagged AirPods case on the table, ask them to tap their phone against it, and let them watch your teammate's screen light up across the room.

That single interaction does the entire pitch. No app, no signup, two seconds.

  1. Set up: teammate's phone on the table showing the owner dashboard, screen awake, page open. This is the receiving end.
  2. Line: “This is a normal AirPods case. There is nothing on the outside.” Let them look.
  3. Judge taps. A notification appears on their phone. They tap it and your page opens in their browser.
  4. They press one button and choose a building.
  5. The dashboard across the room updates instantly. Point at it.
  6. Land the point: the finder never saw who owns it, the owner never saw who found it, and nobody installed anything.
  7. Then the evidence: phones return at 82.8% and umbrellas at 1.4% because phones can say whose they are. This makes everything else behave like a phone.
Record a video at hour six

Film the working demo while it works. If anything fails live, you play the video and keep talking. This costs five minutes and has saved more hackathon presentations than any amount of engineering.

8. What you cannot do today, and should just say

  • Truly unremovable. A sticker is a sticker. Say that real permanence needs embedding at manufacture — in-mould or potted — and that the sticker is the pilot form factor.
  • Washing and abrasion validation. Datasheets show an IP68 inlay surviving 60 wash cycles at 60 °C, but you will not have tested your own assembly. Cite the datasheet, do not claim you verified it.
  • On-metal at small size. Ferrite tags are 38 mm and opaque. Be upfront that steel bottles and laptop lids are a known open problem.
  • Scale of the registry. You will have five tags, not five thousand. The interesting claim is the workflow, not the database.
  • Proof it improves recovery. You have strong analogous evidence — microchipped stray cats are returned at 38.5% against 1.8% — but no trial of your own. Present it as the hypothesis your pilot would test.
