Verdict

You asked whether a tag that sticks to anything — clothes, water bottles, chargers — already exists, and said you are far cheaper than the pet tag. Both halves need correcting. It exists in quantity, and your cost comparison is against the wrong product. PetHub costs $9.95 because it is a machined aluminium pet tag on a collar ring. The products you are actually competing with are printed vinyl stickers with an anonymous relay behind them, and they already sell for about a dollar.

The two facts that should change what you say on stage
  • Students at the University of Virginia are already selling this. Papertags, out of the McIntire School, is a 2″ × 2″ NFC-enabled sticker, customisable with a logo, at $2.50 each with more than 900 sold. Same idea, same customer, already shipping.
  • A student at your own university reportedly did it too. Beacon Tags at WashU was reported as handing out free QR labels to recover misplaced campus items. The article returned HTTP 403 so this is unconfirmed — but find out before a judge does, because if it is true it will be the first question you get.

Source: McIntire School, University of Virginia, UVA students reinventing how lost items find their way home (2026).

What survives all of this

Nothing above touches the finding from the earlier report, and it is still the only defensible one: every product here sells to an individual. None is wired into an institution. Papertags sells you a sticker. It does not know that your university runs a lost-property desk, holds your contact details, and throws away water bottles after three days.

1. Seven products already do exactly this

These are passive stickers with a real service behind the scan — the finder scans, the owner is notified, and in most cases the owner's identity is masked. All verified from the vendor's own product page.

Where your cost claim actually lands

Against PetHub at $9.95 you are roughly ten times cheaper, and that sounds impressive until someone names Letstrack or Boomerang. Against the real market you are competing at parity, and parity on price is not a pitch.

The raw materials say the floor could go lower: a plain paper Avery label is $0.042 each in a 300 pack, a printed NTAG213 sticker is $0.58 at a thousand, and a dumb laminated-vinyl QR asset label is $0.79 at a thousand. But every one of those is just the sticker — no service, no relay, no notification. The dollar you see in the market is the service, not the substrate.

Source: Avery, Product 22806, 2″ square labels, 300 pack (2026) ✓; GoToTags, Printed NFC sticker, NTAG213 — volume pricing (2026) ✓; Strong Asset Tags, QR code asset labels (2026).

2. The clothing-label industry you did not know you were entering

There is a large, mature, cheap market for sticking identity onto clothes. It sells to parents of school children, and to care homes. It has been solving your fabric problem for decades — badly, but cheaply.

Stikins is the uncomfortable one

Read its specification and it is close to the physical product you described: a multipurpose stick-on label for clothes, shoes and hard goods, independently tested to BS EN ISO6330 for 60 washes at 40 °C, surviving washer, tumble dryer and dishwasher. 15p a label.

Mabel's Tag Mates makes the same move from the other side — a stick-on clothing label the vendor also markets as dishwasher-safe on bottles, phones, chargers and school supplies, at 23¢.

So the durable multi-surface sticker at your price point is already a shipping product. It just prints a name instead of resolving a code. That is a software gap, not a materials gap — which is good news for a hackathon, and bad news for a patent.

Source: Stikins, Stikins information and testing (2026); Mabel's Labels, Tag Mates stick-on clothing labels (2026).

The laundry-grade numbers are better than you would guess

Avery Dennison's sew-in UHF fabric label is designed for 50 home wash and dry cycles and passed 100% over 50 cycles at 40 °C with detergent and oxygen bleach. RFIDSU's 15 mm NFC button survives about 200 industrial wash cycles and −30 to +200 °C. SCIVAS will sell you a woven NFC-plus-QR clothing label from $0.11 at a minimum of 100.

Fabric is not your hard problem. It is a solved, purchasable component.

Source: Avery Dennison, AD LoG PFL product information sheet (2026); RFIDSU, PPS button NFC laundry tags (2026); SCIVAS, Washable NFC woven tag (2026).

3. The water bottle: strong problem, one real competitor

First, soften the claim before someone tests it

“The most-lost item on campus is the water bottle” is credible but not universally evidenced. Whitman's caretaker gives the clearest count — a couple hundred water bottles every year, the most consistent item. But Texas A&M, which logs over 12,000 items a year at 50–100 a day, says “so many water bottles” while naming earbuds and headphones as the most common. Say one of the most-lost and you cannot be contradicted.

Source: The Pioneer via UWire, Exploring Whitman's lost and found (2015); The Battalion, Texas A&M, A new home for what lost its home (2023).

The disposal evidence, by contrast, is overwhelming and is the better argument anyway. Five institutions, in their own published words:

Reframe the pitch around this, not around frequency

Boise State's policy table gives the disposition of a water bottle as “Will Not Accept — Immediate Disposal”. Virginia Tech lists drink containers under items it does not accept at all.

That is not a storage problem, it is an identification problem. Nobody bins a labelled item. A two-cent label converts a bottle from refuse-on-arrival into a two-second lookup — and Texas A&M already reunites 573/1675 of what it does accept, about 34%, when it has something to work with.

Source: Boise State University, Policy 12160, lost and found property (2024); Virginia Tech, Lost and found (2026); The Battalion, Texas A&M, A new home for what lost its home (2023).

Citron is your closest direct competitor and you should cite it

Citron, a UAE drinkware brand, moulds a QR code into the bottom of the bottle. The owner registers at findmycitron.com; a finder scans; the owner is notified; contact details are exchanged only if both sides agree. That is your privacy model, already shipping, on the exact object you named.

Two things limit it, and both are your opening: it covers only Citron's own 250 ml and 350 ml bottles, and it is a brand feature rather than a label you can apply to the bottle you already own. Nalgene, YETI and CamelBak offer engraving and custom printing but no recovery scheme at all — Nalgene's guarantee explicitly excludes loss.

Source: Citron, Smart QR technology (2026); Nalgene, Frequently asked questions and lifetime guarantee (2026); YETI, Rambler customisation FAQ (2026).

4. One tag will not cover every surface. Plan for three.

This is the part that will bite a one-day build, and it is physics rather than product design.

Three constraints
  • 72 hours to full bond. 300LSE, 468MP and 9471LE all build strength over three days. Apply your demo labels now, not tomorrow.
  • Metal kills plain NFC. A stainless bottle needs an on-metal tag with a ferrite layer: Seritag's 29 mm on-metal NTAG213 is 0.32 mm overall, reads at only 21–25 mm, and costs $1.06 in single units, falling to $0.81 at a thousand.
  • Nobody publishes a dishwasher test. 3M publishes water immersion and humidity data, not dishwasher cycles. Several tag vendors claim dishwasher safety; that is marketing. Do not repeat it as a specification.

Source: Seritag, 29 mm on-metal NTAG213 (2026); 3M, 300LSE technical datasheet (2026).

So what do you actually build

Accept three formats rather than pretending one label does everything. Hard smooth surfaces — laptop, plastic bottle, charger, notebook — take a laminated vinyl QR at well under a dollar. Fabric takes a woven or iron-on tag, and you can buy an NFC-plus-QR one from $0.11. Stainless steel needs an on-metal NFC tag at about $0.81, or simply a printed QR, which does not care what it is stuck to.

The QR does not have the metal problem at all. For a weekend build that is a decisive advantage over NFC, and it is worth saying out loud rather than treating QR as the fallback.

Source: Seritag, 29 mm on-metal NTAG213 (2026); SCIVAS, Washable NFC woven tag (2026); Strong Asset Tags, QR code asset labels (2026).

5. Where this leaves the pitch

The honest, and much stronger, framing

Stop leading with the tag. Lead with the institution.

“Anyone can sell a student a QR sticker — students at UVA already have, nine hundred of them. The sticker is not the product. The product is that Virginia Tech refuses to accept a water bottle, and Boise State bins it on arrival, while both universities hold the contact details of the person who dropped it. We close that gap. The label is just the cheapest way to carry the identifier.”

That version cannot be beaten by naming a competitor, because none of them is trying to do it.

Source: McIntire School, University of Virginia, UVA students reinventing how lost items find their way home (2026); Virginia Tech, Lost and found (2026); Boise State University, Policy 12160, lost and found property (2024).

Limitations

  • Almost everything here is vendor marketing. No independent test of any consumer tag's durability or recovery rate was found. Wash-cycle and adhesion numbers from 3M, Avery Dennison and Stikins are the exceptions, being tied to named standards.
  • The Beacon Tags WashU claim is unconfirmed. The article returned HTTP 403. Treat it as a lead to check locally, not a fact.
  • Prices mix currencies. GBP figures are transcribed as published; dollar conversions in the chart are approximate.
  • Per-unit prices assume the pack size the vendor sells. Several generic asset-label prices require 500 or 1,000 units.
  • Sources that could not be opened, and from which nothing is asserted: Hydro Flask customise page (HTTP 403); Stanley Create pricing (feed exposed no prices); Petit-Fernand (fetch failed, search snippet only); YETI QR-engraving incompatibility (snippet only, not read in page); Beacon Tags / KSDK WashU article (HTTP 403); Amazon marketplace listings (pages inaccessible); 3M published minimum bend radius (none found).
