The short answer

Two different questions hide inside “what gets lost most”, and they have different answers. What is left behind most often is paperwork, clothing and umbrellas. What people care about and come back for is phones, wallets and documents. The gap between those two lists is the entire opportunity.

The pattern, stated once

Return rate does not track how much an item is worth. It tracks whether the item can be connected to a person and whether that person knows where to look. Phones and wallets carry identity. Umbrellas and jumpers do not.

This report is mostly a demonstration that the same pattern appears in every dataset examined, in four countries, across transport, airports, campuses and police forces.

1. The best dataset in the world: Tokyo

Japan's lost-property law requires found items to be handed to police, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Police publish the resulting statistics annually by category. In calendar year 2025 they recorded 4,538,244 found-item reports covering 4,850,775 individual items, plus ¥4.51 billion in cash. Separately, 1,100,657 loss reports were filed by people who had lost something.

Look at what this says about value

Precious metals return at 1.8%. Mobile phones return at 82.8%. The jewellery is worth more. It comes back forty-six times less often.

A ring cannot tell anyone whose it is, and its owner often does not know where they lost it. A phone rings, shows a lock screen, is missed within minutes and is tied to an account. Value is almost irrelevant; identity and traceability are everything.

2. Transport, where the volumes are

Transport systems generate lost property at industrial scale, because they move people between places while they are carrying everything they own.

The TfL and Tokyo datasets were collected seven years apart, in different countries, under different laws, by different kinds of organisation. They produce the same ranking: phones and wallets at the top, umbrellas and jewellery at the bottom.

Being handed in is not the same as arriving

The MTA Inspector General ran a controlled test in 2024: investigators handed 24 items to New York City Transit employees. 20 of the 24 (83%) were never logged and never reached the Lost Property Unit. A 2006–07 test found 23 of 26 (88.5%) missing. On buses, only 2 of 14 items handed to operators were logged.

So the pipeline leaks badly before the desk. But the same audit records the counter-example: an investigator's keychain carrying an email label reached the unit, and staff used the label to contact the owner immediately.

Source: MTA Office of the Inspector General, NYCT Lost and Found Operation Needs Significant Improvement (report 2025-06) (2025).

3. Campus

Campus lost property is poorly documented compared with transport, and almost all of it comes from student newspapers interviewing the lost-property office rather than from published statistics. The volumes are nonetheless substantial and the return rates cluster tightly around one in three.

The campus answer is the water bottle

Five institutions on three continents independently name the same item first. The University of Toronto lists water bottles, umbrellas, keys, binders and books as most frequently lost. McGill's library calls water bottles “the most popular”. Whitman College collects a couple of hundred a year. Indiana University's article is literally titled “Lost your keys? Missing a water bottle?”. UT Austin's Gregory Gym alone takes in over 100 water bottles a week.

Then look at what institutions do with them. Exeter disposes of water bottles immediately rather than storing them for 30 days like everything else. UNSW does the same. Manchester Metropolitan classifies bottles, clothing and umbrellas as low value and holds them a week against a month for electronics.

So the single most commonly lost object on a campus is one that lost-property offices bin on arrival because storing it is not worth the shelf space. That is the clearest addressable gap in this entire report.

Source: University of Toronto, Exploring lost and found on campus (2023); McGill University Library, In search of a single boot: things lost and found at McGill Library (2021); Indiana University, Lost your keys? Missing a water bottle? (2018); The Daily Texan (UT Austin), Gregory Gym should not throw away reusable water bottles (2018); University of Exeter, Lost property (2026); University of New South Wales, Lost property (2026); Manchester Metropolitan University, Lost property policy (2025).

The rest of the campus list is consistent across institutions: ID cards, keys, chargers and cables, clothing, headphones, laptops, umbrellas, glasses and calculators. A one-day snapshot of a single Bates College library cubby held 42 items including two water bottles, six earbud cases (four of them empty), two graphing calculators, two charging cables, a Bose headset, an umbrella, two pairs of glasses and two rings. Indiana University reports that water bottles and keys have the highest return success while clothing and shoes linger, and that finds spike at the start of term with hats, gloves and scarves arriving in the cold months.

Source: Bates College, 53 items in the Bates library's lost and found (2024); Indiana University, Lost your keys? Missing a water bottle? (2018); Folia (University of Amsterdam), Every year several thousand items are lost at the UvA (2019); Towson University, Personal property registration and labelling guidance (2026).

4. Hotels, venues and the home

Evidence here is dominated by public-relations surveys. The rankings are still useful — they agree with the operational data — but the methodology is usually thin and the denominators are frequently missing. Everything in this section is labelled accordingly.

One exception is a genuinely official venue dataset. The City of Munich publishes lost-property counts for Oktoberfest: 4,095 items in 2024, across a sixteen-day festival.

5. Putting it together

Across every source examined, the picture is consistent enough to state plainly.

The three groups that matter for design
  • Self-identifying, high return. Phones, wallets, documents, ID cards. These already work — 67–83% return. Adding a tag to a phone is solving a solved problem.
  • High volume, near-zero return. Clothing, umbrellas, water bottles, chargers, glasses. Clothing alone is 9.5% of everything handed in to Tokyo police and comes back 4.3% of the time. This is where the addressable loss is, and it is exactly the category a campus lost-property office is drowning in.
  • High value, low return. Jewellery, precious metals, cameras. Painful individually, but only about 2–3% of item volume. Emotionally compelling for a pitch; too rare to build a product on.

The strategic point: the items that need identity most are the cheap ones. That is uncomfortable for a business model, and it is the honest read of the data.

Limitations

  • These datasets count different populations. Tokyo counts items handed to police under a legal duty; TfL counts items reaching its lost property office; TSA counts items left at checkpoints; hotel and home figures are self-reported surveys. They are not directly comparable and no attempt has been made to pool them.
  • Tokyo return rates are approximations. The returned counts come from the processing population (4,750,389 items) rather than the intake population (4,850,775), so category ratios are close but not exact.
  • Japanese law makes Tokyo unusual. A legal duty to hand in found property plus a finder's reward system almost certainly raises hand-in rates above what other countries would see. The category pattern should generalise; the absolute volumes should not.
  • Campus data is mostly journalism. Most campus figures come from student newspapers quoting lost-property staff, not from published statistics. Treat volumes as indicative.
  • Hotel, home, rideshare and venue rankings are public relations. Sample sizes are sometimes given and denominators rarely are. They are included because they agree with the operational data, not because they are independently reliable.
  • Nobody publishes what fraction of lost items are ever found at all. Every figure here starts from an item that already reached a desk. The items left on a train seat and swept into a bin are invisible to all of these datasets.
