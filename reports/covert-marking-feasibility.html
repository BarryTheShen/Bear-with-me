Feasibility Memo · Property Marking · Revised

Feasible — and stronger once you stop hiding it from the thief

An assessment of property marking against five stated requirements: unobtrusive, durable, safe, easy to apply, easy to detect. The chemistry is largely solved and sold commercially. The evidence that a covert mark reduces theft or recovers property is weak to null — but the requirement was unobtrusiveness, not concealment from the offender, and that distinction rescues the idea.

Verdict

Every individual requirement is achievable, and products meeting most of them have been sold to police forces for over twenty years. The idea is sound. What follows is an argument about which half of it to build, and one blocker that no formulation can fix.

Scope correction, and it matters a lot

This memo originally read requirement 1 — “cannot be seen easily” — as concealment from the thief. That was wrong. The requirement is that the mark be unobtrusive: it should not deface a laptop lid or a bike frame. Whether an offender can discover it is explicitly not a constraint.

That reading change is worth more than it sounds, because it removes the single biggest objection to the design. The only mechanism in the entire evaluation literature with a measured effect is visible deterrence. If the offender is allowed to know the item is marked, you can use that mechanism instead of fighting it — and you can attack resale value, which is what every successful intervention in this field has actually done.

What survives the correction, and what does not

No longer a problem: covertness versus deterrence. You can now ship an unobtrusive permanent mark plus an overt indication that the item is marked, which is exactly what the evidence supports and exactly what SmartWater and SelectaDNA already do with their warning stickers.

Still a problem — chemistry: requirement 2 fights requirement 4. A mark that survives washing, solvents and abrasion must be covalently bonded, driven into the fibre, or locked in a highly crosslinked film. All need substrate-specific pretreatment. A one-step pen that goes on anything and rubs off nothing remains an engineering contradiction, and it gets marginally harder when the offender knows where to scrub.

Still the decisive problem — nobody checks. This is unaffected by the correction. A mark only recovers property if somebody reads it, and no published measurement of real-world checking rates exists anywhere. Section 4.

The one-sentence version

Build the mark so it is unobtrusive but announced, aim it at destroying resale value rather than at forensic identification, and spend most of your effort on the thing nobody builds — making somebody actually check.

1. Requirement-by-requirement

Why requirement 2 is genuinely hard

Adhesion comes from three things: a cohesive crosslinked polymer network, chemical or mechanical coupling into the substrate, and immobilisation of the taggant inside that network. On glass or anodised aluminium silanes can condense to real Si–O–M bonds — but laptop lids are usually clear-coated or oily, which defeats coupling. On carbon-fibre and painted frames you are bonding to a clearcoat that itself delaminates. On nylon and polyester a surface film simply abrades and surfactant-extracts; durability needs dyeing-like penetration or grafting onto the fibre, typically with heat or solvent. On polycarbonate the surface energy is low and solvents cause stress cracking.

Etching or roughening would fix adhesion, and simultaneously breaks requirements 1 and 3 by visibly damaging the item.

2. What the chemistry can actually do

With concealment from the offender off the requirement list, your reference case — ordinary UV ink — goes from being the weakest option to being the right one. The objection to it was always that a five-pound 365 nm torch reveals it to a thief as easily as to you. If that does not matter, UV fluorescence wins on every remaining axis: cheapest to make, simplest to apply, and readable with hardware the whole campus already owns.

What still matters chemically is durability. The single most useful intervention available to you is encapsulating the dye rather than dissolving it, which addresses both photobleaching and solvent extraction at once.

The formulation to actually build

An encapsulated UV fluorophore in a clear, crosslinking water-based binder, applied by dauber. Not upconversion, not DNA, not Raman. Those buy covertness you have now told me you do not need, and each one costs you either a $15,000–21,000 reader or a laboratory turnaround.

The encapsulation is the part worth engineering. Dissolving a dye in a binder gives you something that photobleaches and washes out; locking the same dye inside silica nanoparticles cut UV-driven intensity loss from 73% to 13.5% over an hour of exposure and retained 92% of fluorescence after 240 hours immersed in water. That is a real, measured, replicable durability gain and it is the defensible technical contribution in this project.

Read it with a 365 nm torch and an unmodified phone camera. Both are already in every pocket and every campus security office.

Source: Wuhan University, Fluorescent silica nanoparticle anti-counterfeiting ink (Materials 13) (2020); PMC, Smartphones as near-infrared detectors: a review (2021).

3. Does marking actually work? Read this before building

This is where the proposal runs into trouble. Property marking has been trialled since the 1970s, and the better the study design, the smaller the effect.

The recovery evidence is worse than the deterrence evidence

Deterrence at least produces the occasional positive result. Recovery — the thing your ink is actually for — produces almost nothing:

  • South Wales, Home Office 1985: zero stolen goods returned to owners. Of 21 detections in the trial period, none involved marked goods. Only four marked items were taken in participant burglaries at all.
  • Operation Identification, United States: the multi-site evaluation found participant burglary risk down 32.8%, but participation reached only about 7% in the best sector, the contribution of marking could not be separated from the other programme activities, and there was no documented appreciable recovery or return of property attributable to the scheme. Property-recovery measures showed no significant results.
  • Sweden: no effect on burglary or on clearance.

Three separate national programmes, decades apart, all failed at the specific job you are designing for.

Source: Laycock, UK Home Office, Property Marking: A Deterrent to Domestic Burglary? (Crime Prevention Unit Paper 3) (1985); US Department of Justice / National Institute of Justice, Evaluation of Operation Identification, Volume 2 (NCJ 28908) (1975); Kyvsgaard, Ribe & Sorensen, Does Chemical Property Marking Deter Burglary? Results from a New Danish Experiment (Cambridge Journal of Evidence-Based Policing 6:226) (2022) ✓.
The sticker finding is now your best asset, not your problem

The Danish review states it directly: prior research measures warning stickers, not the marks themselves — and there is no reason to expect deterrence if offenders are unaware the property is marked. Under the original covert reading, that was fatal: an invisible mark deters nobody.

Under the corrected reading it is the opposite. The one component with any measured effect is the component you are now allowed to include. Ship the unobtrusive permanent mark and an overt, tamper-evident indication that the item carries it. That is not a compromise of the design; it is the design the evidence actually supports.

Two cautions. The measured sticker effects were short-lived, faded within twelve months, and in West Bromwich displaced theft to the adjacent area. And the retail-tagging review's finding that conspicuous beats inconspicuous is about retail tags with alarms attached. Neither result licenses a strong claim; both point the same direction.

Source: Kyvsgaard, Ribe & Sorensen, Does Chemical Property Marking Deter Burglary? Results from a New Danish Experiment (Cambridge Journal of Evidence-Based Policing 6:226) (2022) ✓; Chainey, Evaluating the effectiveness of forensic marking (Security Journal) (2021); UK College of Policing, Crime Reduction Toolkit — Retail tagging to prevent shop theft (2017) ✓.

For calibration: the College of Policing rates 82 interventions in its Crime Reduction Toolkit, and property marking is not one of them — there is no systematic review of sufficient quality to rate. The nearest rated analogue is retail tagging, which comes out as “mixed findings” on strong evidence: no statistically significant overall effect on crime. And crucially, that review found conspicuous tags outperformed inconspicuous ones.

Source: UK College of Policing, Crime Reduction Toolkit (82 rated interventions) (2026) ✓; UK College of Policing, Crime Reduction Toolkit — Retail tagging to prevent shop theft (2017) ✓; UK College of Policing, Interventions — situational crime prevention (neighbourhood crime) (2022).

4. The blocker nobody costs in: who checks?

Suppose the ink is perfect. Invisible, indestructible, harmless, one-swipe, readable with a two-pound torch. It still does nothing unless somebody looks.

You already have the decisive data point from earlier research

Indiana University found that 36% of stolen bicycles were registered and 10% were recovered. Registration was not the constraint. Nobody checking the registry was the constraint.

Motor vehicles recover at 56% of value and everything else at about 8% — not because cars are better marked, but because a VIN is checked automatically and routinely at registration, insurance, resale and traffic stop. The mark is the easy half. The mandatory, habitual check is the half that actually produces recovery, and it is institutional rather than chemical.

Source: Indiana University, Bike theft survey results (2024).

5. Safety and regulation are not a formality

  • ‘Harmless’ is a regulated claim. Under the US Federal Hazardous Substances Act, deceptive ‘safe’ or ‘harmless’ labelling is prohibited outright, and any irritant or strong sensitiser triggers mandatory labelling. In the EU, CLP requires classification and labelling before you may place a chemical on the market.
  • The market leader is not harmless. The SmartWater safety data sheet lists benzisothiazolinone at 0.030% — a Category 1 skin sensitiser (H317), with an EUH208 allergy warning, and recommends gloves and eye protection.
  • A common UV marker pen is worse. The Sirchie UV700 data sheet classifies it as highly flammable, harmful if swallowed, a skin irritant, causing serious eye damage, and causing drowsiness. Solvent-based invisible inks are not benign.
  • Avoid an aerosol or spray format. NIOSH guidance treats spraying and sonicating engineered nanomaterials as emission tasks requiring containment and local exhaust; liquid suspensions applied by pen or dauber have a far lower exposure profile and a far lighter regulatory burden.
  • Avoid cadmium quantum dots outright. The EU RoHS general limit is 0.01% by weight in any homogeneous material, and the quantum-dot exemptions are narrow and display-specific. The international chemical safety card for cadmium sulfide classifies it as carcinogenic to humans, warns that harmful airborne concentrations are reached quickly once the powder is dispersed, and the EU occupational limit is 0.001 mg/m³ inhalable. That is not a student-handled consumer product.
  • Check your fluorophore, not just your binder. Rhodamine B, a common fluorescent dye, carries aggregated industry classifications of H318 serious eye damage (98.2% of 2,557 reports) and long-lasting aquatic toxicity. Fluorescence and benignity are unrelated properties.
  • UV torches carry their own risk. The FDA measured some handheld UV-C wands emitting up to 3,000 times the ICNIRP exposure limit at two inches. Specify 365 nm UV-A, never UV-C, and say so in your documentation.

Source: US CPSC, FHSA requirements for hazardous substances (2026); SmartWater Technology Research Ltd, SmartWater safety data sheet (2017); Sirchie, UV700 invisible ink safety data sheet (2016); US NIOSH, General Safe Practices for Working with Engineered Nanomaterials (2012); European Union, RoHS Directive consolidated text (2024); IPCS / WHO / ILO, International Chemical Safety Card 0404 — cadmium sulfide (2018); US National Institutes of Health, PubChem, Rhodamine B — aggregated GHS classifications (2026); US FDA, UV wands can give unsafe levels of radiation (2022).

6. What already exists, and what it costs

Before building, note that the invisible-plus-UV-plus-database product has been on sale for two decades.

On the durability claims you will see quoted

Selectamark's products are listed by Secured by Design, the UK police security accreditation body, as tested to PAS 820:2012 with artificial weathering rated Grade A (over five years) under a UKAS-accredited laboratory certificate. That is a real accreditation and worth more than a marketing page.

Two caveats before you treat it as settled. It is commissioned artificial weathering, not independent field testing against deliberate removal by someone who knows the mark is there. And a security-product accreditation is not a toxicological clearance — the two say nothing about each other. Source: Secured by Design / Police Crime Prevention Initiatives, Selectamark accredited product listing (PAS 820:2012) (2026).

7. What to build

The research is not telling you the idea is bad. It is telling you that the chemistry half is already solved and commoditised, and that the mechanism you should be aiming at is not forensic identification but resale devaluation — the one lever that has worked every time it has been tried.

Aim at disposability, because that is what actually works

Three independent natural experiments from the earlier research in this project share one mechanism, and none of them is forensic:

  • Apple made stolen iPhones unusable → iPhone robberies fell 38% in San Francisco while Samsung thefts rose 12%.
  • UW-Madison made stolen textbooks hard to sell → textbook theft fell 86%.
  • The UK made stolen metal hard to sell for cash → metal theft roughly halved.

None prevented a theft attempt. All three removed the buyer. A mark the offender can see does exactly this: a permanent, visibly institution-owned item is worth less to a buyer, and the offender knows it before taking it. A mark the offender cannot see does none of it. Your clarification moves the product from the mechanism that fails to the mechanism that works.

The strongest version of your idea

Make the check free and automatic at the points where stolen campus property surfaces, and use marking only as the identity layer that makes the check possible.

Concretely: mark items at enrolment with something cheap and covert; then put readers where recovered and resold property actually appears — the campus lost property office, the bike compound, the second-hand marketplace the students themselves use, campus security's found-property intake. The evidence in this memo says the mark alone changes nothing. A mark plus a habitual check is precisely the arrangement that makes stolen cars recover at 56%.

That is also the honest pitch: everyone builds the tag; nobody builds the scanner network, which is why 90% of stolen property is never returned.

Limitations of this memo

  • No independent durability testing exists for any commercial forensic marking product that could be located. Vendor claims of five-year persistence and resistance to removal are unverified, in both directions — they may well be true.
  • The evaluation literature is about burglary, mostly domestic, mostly in Denmark and the UK. No trial of property marking against campus opportunistic theft was found. It is possible marking performs differently there, though nothing suggests it would.
  • The Danish RCTs suffered low compliance — 29% and 32% posted stickers as instructed. A trial with full compliance might detect an effect the authors could not.
  • Absence of evidence on checking rates is not evidence of absence. Police may check recovered property far more often than the published record shows; the point is that nobody has measured it, so a business case cannot assume it.
  • Chemistry figures are laboratory values. The photobleaching and quantum-yield numbers come from controlled studies on paper or in dispersion, not from marked laptops or bicycles exposed to weather and handling.
