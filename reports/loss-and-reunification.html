Verdict

The pivot is well judged. Loss is a bigger and more tractable problem than theft, and your causal story is broadly right — with one important correction and one gap you should know about before pitching it.

The correction: identifiable is not the same as contactable

Your model has one step where the evidence says there are two. When shelters scanned a microchipped stray and still failed to reach the owner, the reasons were measured: 35.4% incorrect or disconnected phone number, 24.3% owner did not respond, 17.2% registered to a different organisation.

So the chain is found → willing → identifiable → contact route still live → owner responds. A permanent mark fixes link three and does nothing for links four and five. An ink that resolves to a phone number a student changed two years ago is a mark that works and a return that fails.

Source: Lord, Ingwersen, Gray & Wintz, Characterization of animals with microchips entering animal shelters (JAVMA 235:160) (2009).
And the honest gap you should say out loud

Every major finder experiment supplies the finder with contact details. Cohn created a unique email address for every one of the 17,303 wallets. The USB study used labelled drives and resumes. The 2025 Chinese wallet study put a visible QR sticker on all 660 wallets.

These establish the ceiling: when a finder can contact the owner, 40–72% do. None of them measures what happens when they cannot, because no experiment has run that arm. If you claim a specific recovery uplift, you are extrapolating. Say so, and cite the pet data as your analogue.

Source: Cohn, Marechal, Tannenbaum & Zund, Civic honesty around the globe (Science 365:70-73) (2019) ✓; Tischer et al., Users Really Do Plug in USB Drives They Find (IEEE S&P) (2016); Han et al., Nudging civic honesty: a QR-mediated lost-wallet field experiment (Scientific Reports 15) (2025).

1. Links (a) and (b): finders exist, and they try

The volume side is not in doubt, and neither is willingness. What is striking is how consistently willingness lands in the same band across wildly different methods, countries and decades.

The volumes are equally clear. Of 297 USB drives dropped around a university campus, 290 were picked up. Institutions are drowning in found property: TSA handles 90,000–100,000 items left at checkpoints every month; the University of Washington's student union processes over 1,000 items a month; Transport for London received 333,921 items in 2018/19.

Source: Tischer et al., Users Really Do Plug in USB Drives They Find (IEEE S&P) (2016); US Transportation Security Administration, Lost and found (2026); University of Washington, HUB Lost and Found policy A-12 (2025); Transport for London, Lost Property Office transparency data FY2017-18-19 (2018/19) ✓.

Two honest deductions from the same data

Willingness is not the same as follow-through. In the USB study 68% said they intended to return the drive, but only 54 of 297 drives (18%) were physically handed back. And in the Perth letter experiment, adding a small cost to the finder — making them buy a stamp — cut returns from 61.3% to 30.7%.

Friction halves it. Whatever you build has to be a single, obvious, free action.

Source: Tischer et al., Users Really Do Plug in USB Drives They Find (IEEE S&P) (2016); Grueter et al., Are stamped letters returned? A lost-letter experiment (PeerJ) (2016).

2. Link (c): does identity actually drive reunification?

No object experiment has tested this. The closest large-sample real-world test is animals, and it is a good analogue: a creature that cannot say who owns it, made identifiable by a cheap implanted tag, in a system that routinely scans.

The same study measured how often the owner was found at all, broken down by whether usable registration existed. This is the cleanest available statement of your hypothesis:

A finding that cuts against the simple version of your story

A survey of 156 people who found a pet reported that only 10% of reunifications were attributed to information on the animal's tag. A national US owner survey found 93% of lost dogs and 75% of lost cats were recovered, but only 15% of dog recoveries and 2% of cat recoveries happened because someone was contacted via tag or microchip.

Most reunifications happen by the owner searching, not the finder calling. That does not refute your premise, but it reframes the size of the prize: identity marking is not the main channel today. Your bet is that it could be, and that is a defensible bet — the pet data shows the marked-and-registered subgroup does far better. Just do not claim it is how recovery normally works.

Source: Rajala-Schultz et al., Reunifying lost pets with their owners (JAVMA 230:1835) (2007); Weiss, Slater & Lord, Search methods used to locate missing cats and dogs (Animals) (2012).

3. How lost-and-found actually works, and why that matters

A crucial operational finding: almost every published lost-property workflow is owner-initiated. The owner searches; the institution matches. Nobody goes looking for the owner.

Fresno State is your product, in miniature

Fresno State's own procedures manual says a found university ID card is forwarded to the ID office so that office can notify the person. Everything else — phones, keys — is destroyed after a week or auctioned after three months.

The institution already runs a working reunification pipeline. It applies to exactly one object, because that is the only object with a campus identity on it. Your entire product is extending that pipeline to everything else a student carries. That is a far stronger pitch than a new ink, and it is evidenced.

Source: Fresno State, Property and Equipment Procedures Manual (2024).

4. What the pivot does to the ink

It inverts the first requirement and promotes the fifth. In the theft case there is an adversary who must not find the mark. In the loss case there is no adversary at all — the only person who will ever look for your mark is someone trying to help. Hiding it from them is working against yourself.

The measurement that settles it

A spectral study of smartphone flashes across four handsets (iPhone 6s, 8, XR and Samsung Note8) found they are phosphor-converted white LEDs peaking at 435–455 nm with a broad 500–700 nm band. There is no 365 nm output.

So an unmodified phone cannot excite an ordinary UV-fluorescent mark. A finder in a café would need to own a dedicated 365 nm torch and think to sweep your water bottle with it. No research quantifies how many people would do that, and the honest answer is approximately none.

To be precise about the physics: the phone's camera can photograph visible fluorescence perfectly well if something else supplies the excitation. The failure is the light source, not the sensor.

Source: Vu et al., Smartphone flash spectral characterisation (Biosensors 12:1060) (2022) ✓.

What replaces it

An overt, low-disclosure marker resolving through a proxy. The evidence for each component:

  • Visible contact affordance works. In the 2025 Chinese field experiment, 660 wallets each carried a visible sticker reading “Scan the QR code to contact the owner”. 66.3% of recipients scanned it and 59.3% phoned the owner. The wallets were handed to reception staff rather than found in the wild, so this is not a population finder rate — but it shows the affordance is used when present.
  • Never print the actual contact details. The Washington State Attorney General explicitly advises against putting a home address on a luggage tag, because it tells a thief where the empty house is. Use a code that resolves through your service and relays the message; reveal nothing about the owner.
  • Durable visible marking on textiles is a solved problem. QR codes sublimated onto polyester at 20 mm or larger stayed 100% readable through five 40 °C wash and tumble-dry cycles; heat-transfer at 30–50 mm likewise. Embroidered codes failed. Separately, screen-printed tags on cotton and polyester survived 7,500 Martindale abrasion cycles with under 20% erosion and ten 60 °C wash cycles with as little as 2% erosion.
  • Do not assume scanning is automatic. A naturalistic study observed 7,356 people entering a university library past a displayed QR code: 58 approached and only 22 scanned it — 0.30%. That was an unmotivated poster rather than a found object, and motive plainly matters, but it is a warning against assuming a code gets used simply because it exists.
  • Keep a covert mark only as a secondary layer. It has one real use in the loss case: proving ownership in a dispute over an unlabelled item, and surviving after a visible label is scuffed off. It should never be the primary discovery route.

Source: Han et al., Nudging civic honesty: a QR-mediated lost-wallet field experiment (Scientific Reports 15) (2025); Washington State Attorney General, Travel tips: luggage tags (2026); Hasan, Kaunas University of Technology, Durability of QR codes applied to polyester textiles (MSc thesis) (2026); Koehl, Agrawal & Campagne, Secured tag printing on textile substrates (Int J Adv Manuf Technol 102) (2019); Ball, Warmelink, Nightingale & Crawford, Naturalistic observation of public QR code scanning (iJIM 20:14) (2026).

5. NFC: how it works, and what it can and cannot be

NFC resolves the aesthetic objection better than anything else available, because it needs no visible marking at all. But the physics imposes hard limits on how small it can go, and “unremovable” is the one requirement it cannot meet as a sticker.

How it actually works

  • It is magnetic coupling, not radio. NFC runs at 13.56 MHz ± 7 kHz and operates in the near field. The reader drives an alternating current through a coil, producing an oscillating magnetic field. ISO/IEC 14443-2 requires the reader to produce between 1.5 and 7.5 A/m at the tag.
  • The tag has no battery and no transmitter. The tag is a coil plus a chip. The reader's changing magnetic field induces a voltage in the tag's coil by Faraday induction; the chip rectifies and regulates that to power itself. The reader's field is literally the tag's power supply.
  • The tag answers by changing how much power it draws. This is load modulation: the tag switches a load across its own coil, which changes the impedance the reader sees in its coil. For Type A the tag does this against a subcarrier at fc/16 = 847.5 kHz. Nothing is transmitted; the reader is detecting a tiny disturbance in its own field.
  • This is why range is centimetres and why size matters so much. In the near field the coupling falls off roughly as the cube of distance, and the mutual inductance between the two coils scales with coil area and turns. Halving the tag's dimensions does far more damage than intuition suggests.
  • Tuning matters. The coil and its capacitance form an LC circuit resonant near 13.56 MHz. NXP's design guidance gives typical antenna values of 0.3–3 µH inductance and quality factor Q of 20–35. Anything that detunes the circuit — principally metal — destroys power transfer.
  • The data format is NDEF. A URI record holds a URL. That is what makes a phone offer to open your page.

Source: ISO/IEC, ISO/IEC 14443-2:2016 — Radio frequency power and signal interface (2016) ✓; NXP Semiconductors, AN11564 — NFC antenna design guide (2016); NXP Semiconductors, NTAG213/215/216 product data sheet (2015).

Can it be tiny, flat, soft and invisible?

The one that will bite you: metal

An NFC tag stuck to aluminium or steel stops working. A conductive surface near the coil develops eddy currents that oppose the reader's field, absorb power and detune the resonant circuit. NXP's own antenna design note describes exactly this mechanism.

The fix is a ferrite layer between tag and metal to redirect the flux. That works, but it makes the tag bigger, thicker and opaque: a typical on-metal NFC label carries a 35 mm antenna in a 38 mm die-cut with an opaque face.

For your target items this splits cleanly. AirPods cases, backpacks, notebooks and plastic bottles are fine — they are non-conductive, and your own example is the best case of all. Aluminium laptop lids and steel water bottles are the hard ones, and there the honest answer is either an on-metal tag on the plastic underside, or a different marking method entirely.

Source: NXP Semiconductors, AN11564 — NFC antenna design guide (2016); Avery Dennison, AD Bullseye On-Metal data sheet (2026).
The part that makes this work: no app required

Apple's Core NFC documentation confirms that iPhone XS and later read NDEF tags in the background with no app installed. The system reads the first URI record, shows a notification, and the user taps it to open the link. It will not fire while the camera or Apple Pay is in use, in airplane mode, or before the phone has been unlocked once.

Android reads NDEF tags when the screen is unlocked and dispatches the URI by intent; if no installed app claims it, behaviour falls back to the browser and, from Android 17, requires an explicit interaction to open a web link.

So a finder taps a phone against an AirPods case and gets a notification. That is the entire interaction, and it needs no visible marking, no app and no account. It is a materially better user experience than a QR code, which requires the finder to see a code, open the camera and frame it.

Source: Apple, Adding support for background tag reading (Core NFC) (2026) ✓; Android Developers, NFC basics (2026).
But solve discovery, or none of it matters

NFC removes the visible mark. It does not remove the need for the finder to know there is something to tap. An unmarked AirPods case looks exactly like an unmarked AirPods case.

Two ways out, and you should probably do both. First, a very small discreet symbol — a few millimetres, in the conventional place manufacturers already put regulatory marks — signalling that the item is tappable. Second, and more reliably, train the institution: campus lost-property desks tap everything on intake as a matter of routine. The second path does not depend on public awareness at all, and the desks already inspect items for names by hand.

Cost is not the obstacle: clear NTAG213 inlays list at roughly $0.14–0.16 each at volume.

Source: GoToTags, Clear wet NFC inlay NTAG213 20 x 10 mm (2026); Fresno State, Property and Equipment Procedures Manual (2024).

6. What to build

The design the evidence points at

A visible, durable, low-disclosure identity marker bound to the campus ID system, plus a proactive notification pipeline in the lost-property office.

Concretely, four parts:

  1. Mark — an overt printed or sublimated code, not covert ink. It says only “found me? scan” and a short code. No name, no number, no address.
  2. Resolve — the code resolves to a relay page that lets the finder notify the owner without seeing who they are, and tells them the nearest hand-in point. One tap, no app, no account.
  3. Bind to a live record — this is the part the pet data says everyone gets wrong. 35.4% of failures were dead phone numbers. Binding to a university account that the registrar already keeps current eliminates that failure mode entirely, and no consumer tag service can match it.
  4. Push, do not wait — make the lost-property office scan intake and notify automatically, the way Fresno State already does for ID cards and the way airlines do for bags. Every workflow examined here is owner-initiated; that is the gap.

Limitations

  • Link (c) is not directly evidenced and cannot honestly be presented as if it were. No experiment has compared identifiable with unidentifiable objects. The pet analogue is strong but it is an analogue.
  • The microchip comparison is confounded by selection. Owners who microchip are plausibly more attached and more likely to search, and the figures are shelter-level medians rather than individual-level matched comparisons.
  • The Chinese wallet study handed items to reception staff at institutions — pharmacies, museums, hotels, police stations. Those are unusually conscientious finders in an unusually clear situation. It is not a coffee-shop stranger rate.
  • Lost-letter studies are a proxy, not lost property. Posting an addressed letter is a much lower-effort act than tracking down the owner of a water bottle.
  • Stated intent overstates behaviour. 68% of USB finders said they meant to return the drive; 18% of drives came back.
  • The QR scanning study measured an unmotivated public poster, not a found object. Its 0.30% should be read as a caution about assuming scans, not as a forecast of your conversion rate.
  • Textile durability results are from two studies on woven cotton and polyester. Knits, nonwovens, coated fabrics and dry cleaning are untested.
  • No operator publishes the fraction of found items carrying identification, which is the single statistic that would let you size this market precisely. It does not appear to exist.
