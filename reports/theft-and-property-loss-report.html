Executive summary

Property leaves its owner through two channels — misplacement and theft — and the instinct is to treat them as different problems. The data says they are largely the same problem wearing two hats. In both channels the dominant mechanism is a small, portable, unmarked object left briefly without a guardian. In both channels the item usually still physically exists afterwards. And in both channels it fails to come home for the same reason: nothing on the object connects it to its owner, and no one who finds it has a cheap way to act.

The central finding

Recovery rates do not track how valuable an item is. They track how identifiable it is. Stolen motor vehicles — registered, numbered, title-tracked — recover at 56.1% of value. Everything else recovers at 8.3%. Jewellery, the second most valuable category after vehicles, recovers at 3.5%. Inside TfL's lost property system the same pattern repeats without any thief involved: phones are reclaimed at 48.3% and umbrellas at 2.3%.

That is the design brief. The binding constraint on getting property back is not surveillance, and not police capacity. It is the absence of a cheap, universal link between an object and its owner, plus somewhere for a finder to discharge it.

What the evidence supports

  1. Opportunity, not skill, drives loss. Campus police put theft of unattended or unsecured property at over half of all crime on their patch; a detailed campus study found 69% of thefts were from unattended bags. Pickpocketing — the archetype — is 0.6% of US larceny.
  2. The criminal justice funnel leaks almost everything. Of US property victimisations, 29.9% are reported, 18.4% of recorded larcenies are cleared, and outside vehicles most value is never recovered. Any solution that routes through policing inherits this funnel.
  3. Situational measures work, but only in narrow, specifiable conditions. CCTV reduces crime ~12% pooled, ~37% in car parks, and not at all when passively monitored. Effects concentrate in property crime, never violent crime.
  4. Killing the resale market beats catching thieves. When Apple shipped Activation Lock, iPhone robberies in San Francisco fell 38% while Samsung thefts rose 12%. Making an object worthless to a stranger is the strongest single lever in the record.
  5. Finders are far more honest than anyone predicts. In a 40-country experiment with 17,303 wallets, adding money increased return rates from 40% to 51%, and large sums pushed it to 72%. Both economists and the public predicted the opposite.
  6. Displacement is the exception, not the rule. Across 572 tests, crime displacement appeared in 26% and diffusion of benefits in 27%. Prevention rarely just moves crime.

1. The two channels: misplacement and theft

A useful taxonomy has to be built around mechanism, because mechanism is what a solution attaches to. Splitting by legal category (theft vs. loss) puts a bright line through the middle of what is actually one continuum of failed guardianship.

The fourth channel is worth a glance precisely because it is the one that works. When an airline mishandles a bag, the bag is inside a system that knows it exists, knows who owns it, and is liable for it. The result is a mishandling rate of 6.3 bags per 1,000 passengers, with about 66% of the 33.4 million mishandled bags resolved within 48 hours. Notably, only 8% of mishandling events are true loss or theft — 74% are simply delayed. Custody plus identity plus liability turns a loss problem into a logistics problem.

Source: SITA, Baggage IT Insights press release (WorldTracer data) (2024). SITA is an air-transport industry supplier; these are its WorldTracer operational data weighted to IATA traffic, not independent official statistics.

2. Losing things without a thief

The best public dataset on non-theft loss is Transport for London's lost property transparency release, because it publishes both what was found and what was reclaimed, by category. In 2018/19, 333,921 items were handed in across the London network and 79,861 were reclaimed — 23.9%.

The mix is unglamorous: paper, clothes and bags dominate. But the interesting number is not what gets lost — it is what comes back.

Why phones come back and umbrellas do not

A phone is reclaimed 48.3% of the time. An umbrella, 2.3%. That is a 21-fold difference, and it is not explained by value alone — jewellery is valuable and is reclaimed only 3.4% of the time.

What separates them is two-sided identifiability. A phone announces its owner (it rings, it has a lock screen, its owner knows exactly where they were) and its owner is motivated enough to chase it. Jewellery is valuable but anonymous and its loss is often not noticed at a specific place or time. An umbrella is anonymous and not worth a trip. Recovery needs both a link to the owner and a motivated claimant; value alone supplies only the second.

Time is the enemy of recovery

Lost property systems are not archives. TfL holds ordinary property for 3 months and cash for 12 months, after which unclaimed items are donated to charity or sold at public auction. New York's Metro-North allows 5–7 business days simply for an item to reach the facility, holds property for a minimum of 90 days, and reports that the vast majority of items that are returned are returned within a week. The practical window in which a reunion is possible is short, and it opens before most owners work out where to look.

Source: Transport for London, Lost Property Office transparency data FY2017-18-19 (2018/19); Metropolitan Transportation Authority, Lost and Found — Metro-North Railroad FAQs (2025).

Strangers return things far more often than anyone expects

The single most useful behavioural result for anyone designing a recovery system is the Cohn et al. field experiment published in Science. Researchers turned in 17,303 wallets at banks, museums, post offices, hotels and public offices across 355 cities in 40 countries, varying only whether the wallet contained money, and measured whether the recipient emailed the owner within 100 days.

Three design implications from the wallet experiment
  • Do not assume finders are the problem. Baseline honesty is high and rises with stakes. The bottleneck is contactability, not morality.
  • Adding a key raised reporting by 9.2 percentage points even though a key is worthless to the finder. Signalling that the loss hurts someone motivates return — a lost-item tag should say who it belongs to, not just offer a reward.
  • Both the public and professional economists predicted the opposite result. Intuitions about finder behaviour are unreliable; design against the data.

3. Theft at scale: what the official numbers say

The US Uniform Crime Reporting programme provides the cleanest categorical breakdown of theft anywhere. In 2019 there were an estimated 5,086,096 larceny-thefts nationwide — a rate of 1,549.5 per 100,000 inhabitants, and 73.4% of all property crime.

The pickpocket is a rounding error

Pocket-picking (0.6%) and purse-snatching (0.4%) together are 1.0% of US larceny. Meanwhile theft from motor vehicles (27.0%), theft from buildings (10.0%), shoplifting (22.2%) and the residual 'all other larceny' (29.9%) — overwhelmingly thefts of items left somewhere — make up the bulk of the total.

Public imagination, and a lot of product design, is aimed at the skilled thief working a crowd. The actual adversary is a person who walks past an unattended thing. Any hackathon solution optimised for defeating a professional pickpocket is optimising against 1% of the problem.

Most theft never reaches the police at all

Police-recorded statistics measure reporting behaviour as much as criminal behaviour. The US National Crime Victimization Survey interviews households directly and therefore counts crimes the police never hear about. In 2023 it estimated 13,637,450 property victimisations, a rate of 102.2 per 1,000 households — and found that only 29.9% were reported to police, down from 31.8% the previous year.

Reporting rates are an incentive readout

Motor vehicle theft is reported at 72.4%; ordinary theft at 24.8%. The difference is not how upset the victim is. It is that a car has a registration, an insurer, and a paperwork process that requires a police report. Where infrastructure makes reporting worthwhile, people report. The UK data shows the same shape: domestic burglary is reported at 57.7% and 'other theft of personal property' at 25.2%.

The same pattern outside the US

England and Wales recorded a sharp rise in theft from the person: the Crime Survey estimated 347,000 incidents in the year to March 2024 against 247,000 the year before, a 40% increase, while police recorded only 131,453 such offences — roughly 38% of the survey estimate. Across the EU, 5,261,267 thefts were recorded by police in 2024. National rates differ enormously (France 1,956 per 100,000), (Germany 1,410 per 100,000) but Eurostat cautions these reflect differences in law, recording practice and reporting propensity as much as in underlying crime.

Source: UK Office for National Statistics, Crime in England and Wales, year ending March 2024 (2024); Eurostat, Crime statistics — police-recorded offences (2024).

4. The recovery funnel: why property never comes home

Put the numbers in sequence and the picture is stark. Start with 100 property crimes as victims experience them, and follow how many produce a returned object.

Each stage of that funnel is a separate design opportunity, and they are not equally tractable. Raising the clearance rate means more police. Raising the reporting rate means giving victims a reason to bother. Raising the recovery rate means making found property matchable. The last is the only one a small team can move.

The evidence that identity, not value, drives recovery

Why cars are the exception that proves the rule

A car is the most valuable, most attractive, most professionally targeted consumer object most people own. By the CRAVED model it should be the hardest to protect. Yet it recovers at 56% while a laptop recovers with everything else at around 8%.

The difference is a stack of boring infrastructure: a unique visible identifier (VIN and plate), a mandatory owner registry, a title system that makes resale to an innocent buyer difficult, and a police database that is checked routinely rather than on request. Nothing about that stack is technically hard. It simply does not exist for anything smaller than a vehicle.

5. Methods: how strangers actually take things

This section carries a health warning. Theft techniques are extensively catalogued in police guidance and journalism, but most have no defensible prevalence statistic. Official crime statistics record legal categories, not tradecraft: there is no national dataset recording what proportion of bike thefts used an angle grinder. Where a base rate exists it is given; where it does not, the entry is marked No base rate rather than filled with an invented number.

The digital-physical attack chain deserves specific attention

The most consequential modern theft method is not about the object at all. An offender who observes a phone passcode and then steals the phone can change the account password, turn off device location, lock the owner out of their own recovery tools, and reach saved payment credentials. The device is the least valuable thing taken.

Apple's response, Stolen Device Protection (iOS 17.3 and later, and it must be enabled before the theft), is instructive as a design pattern: when the phone is away from familiar locations, sensitive actions require Face ID or Touch ID with no passcode fallback, and critical actions such as changing the account password require biometric authentication, a one-hour security delay, and then biometric authentication again. Find My cannot be switched off while it is active.

The generalisable principle: knowledge of a secret should not be sufficient to sever an object from its owner. Add a delay, a second factor, and a location context. Source: Apple, Use Stolen Device Protection on iPhone (2026).

Which items get targeted, and why

Ronald Clarke's CRAVED model remains the best predictor of what gets stolen: goods are targeted when they are Concealable, Removable, Available, Valuable, Enjoyable and Disposable. The last term is the operative one for solution design. Disposable means there is a working resale channel. Remove disposability and the other five stop mattering — which is exactly what the smartphone kill-switch did, and exactly why vehicles, despite scoring high on every other letter, are recovered so often.

Source: Clarke (Home Office Police Research Series 112), Hot Products: understanding, anticipating and reducing demand for stolen goods (1999).

6. The campus case

Universities are the right first deployment context for a structural reason: they are dense, closed-ish communities with a shared identity system, a shared physical estate, high-value portable electronics, and a population that moves constantly between spaces while carrying everything it owns. They are also, unfortunately, badly measured.

The Clery Act blind spot

US institutions must publish campus crime statistics under the Clery Act, and the primary reportable offence categories include burglary, motor vehicle theft and arson — but ordinary larceny-theft is not a primary Clery-reportable category. It appears only in the hate-crime schedule.

The practical consequence: the single most common crime on campus is structurally invisible in the one dataset that covers every US institution. USC's annual security report, for example, tabulates burglary and motor vehicle theft on campus, but ordinary larceny is absent from its criminal offences table entirely. Any analysis built only on Clery data will systematically miss the actual problem. Source: US Dept of Education, Federal Student Aid, FSA Handbook 2024-25, Appendix E — Institutional Reporting and Disclosure (2024); University of Southern California Dept of Public Safety, Annual Security and Fire Safety Report 2023 (2022).

What institutions that do publish larceny actually see

Georgia Tech's police department publishes a full Part I crime table, larceny included. The result is unambiguous.

This is the number the Clery dataset cannot show you: roughly six in ten campus crimes are ordinary theft. And the University of Miami Police Department states plainly that theft of unattended or unsecured property alone accounts for over half of all crime on its Coral Gables campus.

The best-documented campus theft study

UW-Madison's police department ran a problem-oriented policing project on textbook theft after incidents rose 164% in a year. Its value here is that it recorded mechanism and location, which almost no campus dataset does.

The intervention that worked, and why it is the model

UW-Madison's police did not add cameras or patrols. They identified that stolen textbooks were being resold for cash to local buy-back shops, and pursued a city ordinance regulating second-hand book purchases — requiring seller identification and holding periods.

Result: textbook theft incidents fell 86% and books stolen fell 91% in the comparable half-year after implementation. They attacked disposability — the D in CRAVED — rather than the theft event itself. Source: UW-Madison Police Dept / ASU Center for Problem-Oriented Policing, Textbook Theft Project (Goldstein Award submission) (2008).

The report also notes students frequently did not report thefts because they had no confidence anything would be recovered, which is the reporting-rate problem in microcosm.

Campus bicycle theft: a complete failure case

Indiana University surveyed its community about bike theft in autumn 2024 (590 respondents, 387 of whom had owned a bike in Bloomington in the past five years, yielding 97 reported incidents). The result is a clean picture of a recovery system that does not function.

Registration only helps if it is checked

36% of the stolen bikes were registered, and only 10% were recovered. Registration on its own is not a recovery mechanism — it is only the first half of one. The second half is a routine, low-friction check at the moments a stolen bike surfaces: resale, impound, abandonment, campus racks. Building the registry is the easy part and the part everyone builds.

The seasonality finding is directly actionable for a campus deployment: 22% of incidents fell in August and 20% in September. Intervention effort concentrated on the first six weeks of the academic year addresses roughly two-fifths of the annual problem, at a moment when new students are also most reachable.

7. What currently works, measured honestly

This section reports effect sizes from systematic reviews and meta-analyses rather than vendor claims. Negative values mean less crime. Bars show published confidence intervals; where an interval crosses zero, the intervention did not produce a statistically significant effect no matter how popular it is.

Four rules that fall out of the meta-analyses
  1. Passive cameras do nothing. Actively monitored CCTV reduces crime ~15%; passively monitored CCTV has no significant effect at all. A camera nobody watches is set dressing.
  2. Bundles beat single measures. CCTV deployed with other interventions achieved a ~34% reduction; CCTV alone was not statistically significant.
  3. Enclosed, bounded spaces respond; open ones do not. Car parks ~37%, residential ~12%, city centres not significant. A campus building, bike compound or library floor is much closer to a car park than to a city centre.
  4. These are property-crime tools. Effects on violent crime were not significant in either review. Do not oversell the safety case.

The strongest single case: destroying the resale market

On 18 September 2013 Apple shipped Activation Lock, which renders a stolen iPhone unusable without the owner's credentials. Because Samsung devices lacked an equivalent at the time, the change created a natural comparison.

The mechanism is the one that matters most for a hackathon: Apple did not prevent a single theft attempt. It made the object worthless to the thief. Offenders switched targets rather than persisting — which is displacement, and which is also exactly what you want when the displaced-to target then gets the same protection.

Does prevention just move crime around?

This is the standard objection to every situational measure, and it has been tested at scale. Guerette and Bowers reviewed 102 studies containing 572 separate displacement or diffusion tests.

8. What is popular but weakly evidenced

An honest report has to name the interventions whose evidence does not match their reputation, because these are exactly where a hackathon team will be tempted to go.

A note on source quality encountered during this research

While assembling this report, a smart-locker 'case study' was found attributing specific figures (a 340% rise in package theft, a fall from 127 incidents per semester to 3, a 98% reduction) to a named university in San Diego. No such institution exists. The numbers were excluded.

This is worth stating because the anti-theft technology sector's published evidence base is heavily contaminated with unverifiable and occasionally fabricated case studies. Any claim in this space that does not name a real, checkable institution and a real baseline should be treated as marketing.

9. Design implications and candidate solutions

The evidence converges on a narrow set of principles. What follows ranks candidate solutions by the strength of the mechanism behind them, not by how impressive they are to build.

Five principles the data supports

  1. Attack disposability first. The strongest measured intervention in this entire review is Activation Lock, which changed nothing about theft opportunity and everything about resale value. The UW-Madison ordinance did the same for textbooks. If a stolen thing cannot be converted to money, the theft stops being worth committing.
  2. Give every object a cheap identity, and make checking it free. Vehicles recover at 56% and everything else at 8% because vehicles have identifiers that are checked routinely. Registration alone is not enough — Indiana proved that — the check has to be automatic at the moments an item surfaces.
  3. Design for the honest finder, not the thief. Baseline return rates are 40–72% and rise with the apparent cost to the owner. Most items in the loss channel are already in the hands of someone willing to return them who does not know how. That is a routing problem, and routing problems are tractable.
  4. Target bounded spaces, not open ones. Every significant effect in the meta-analyses came from enclosed, bounded settings. Libraries, gyms, bike compounds and residence halls are the campus equivalents of the car parks where CCTV works.
  5. Reduce the guardianship gap rather than punishing it. The dominant mechanism is a few minutes of absence. Anything that shortens that gap, or substitutes a guardian during it, addresses the majority of incidents.

Candidate solutions, ranked by evidential support

Constraints any student-built system must respect
  • Unwanted tracking. Any Bluetooth-locatable tag falls under the Apple/Google unwanted-tracker specification, currently an IETF Internet-Draft. It sets obligations for small trackers — those under 30cm in one dimension, 18 x 13 cm in two, or 250cm³ — covering advertising behaviour, MAC rotation, non-owner discovery, and the ability for a stalking victim to find and disable the tag. Building a tracker without implementing this is building a stalking device.
  • Broadcast identifiers are abusable. The Send My research demonstrated that arbitrary data can be piggybacked through Apple's Find My network because the BLE public-key broadcasts are neither authenticated nor encrypted. Assume any identifier you broadcast will be logged, replayed and repurposed.
  • Surveillance is not free. Campus CCTV expansion carries data-protection duties and a real community-trust cost, and the meta-analytic evidence does not support open-space camera deployment anyway. Prefer measures that do not require watching people.
  • Privacy in the recovery flow itself. A finder must be able to return an item without learning the owner's name, address or timetable. Route through the institution, never expose the owner directly.
Source: Apple & Google, IETF Internet-Draft (work in progress), Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers (draft-ledvina-apple-google-unwanted-trackers-03) (2026); Positive Security, Send My: arbitrary data transmission via Apple's Find My network (2021).

10. Limitations and evidence gaps

  • The best campus larceny data is old or local. The UW-Madison study dates from 2006–08. It is used here because it is the most methodologically detailed campus theft study located, not because it is current.
  • US larceny composition is from 2019. The FBI's transition from summary UCR to NIBRS disrupted the comparable national series; 2019 is the last clean year for the full nine-category percentage distribution.
  • TfL data is from 2018/19. It is used because it is the only lost-property source located that publishes found and reclaimed counts by category — the pairing that makes the identity finding possible.
  • Method prevalence is largely unmeasured. No official collection reviewed records theft tradecraft. The absence of percentages in Section 5 is a real gap in the world's statistics, and any source offering confident numbers there should be treated with suspicion.
  • Offender composition on campus is unknown. No reliable data on the share of campus theft committed by outsiders versus students, nor on tailgating prevalence, was located in primary sources.
  • UK snatch-theft incidence is suppressed. ONS disclosure control blocks the stealth-versus-snatch incidence split at an unweighted base of 34.
  • The funnel chart composites different collections. Its stages come from a victim survey, police clearance records and an agency property return, across different years and samples. It is a scale diagram, not a cohort study.
  • The Activation Lock evidence is uncontrolled. Large, consistent and directionally convincing — but administrative trend data published by an advocacy initiative, without controls or confidence intervals.
  • The CSEW excludes communal establishments, including student halls of residence, so UK survey figures under-represent exactly the population this report targets.
