Research Report II · Opportunistic Theft

Theft of unattended property: how big is it, really?

Opportunistic theft is not a category in any crime statistic on earth. This report reconstructs its size from the measures that do exist — entry method, physical contact, lock status and campus incident coding — and answers two questions: what share of all theft it represents, and what share of campus theft.

The answer, up front

Opportunistic theft — property taken because it was left unattended or unsecured, with no force and no contact with the owner — is the overwhelming majority of theft, and it is even more dominant on campus than nationally. Two numbers frame it:

Why the answer has to be a range

No crime statistics agency in the US, UK, Australia, Canada or the Netherlands records whether stolen property was left unattended. There is no tick-box for it. This report therefore triangulates from four measures that are collected:

  1. Physical contact — whether the item was taken off the victim's body. Collected by the FBI and by NCVS.
  2. Method of entry — whether force was used. Collected for burglary by the FBI and for burglary and vehicles by the UK's Crime Survey.
  3. Lock status — whether the vehicle or bicycle was locked. Collected only by the UK.
  4. Offence category definition — the UK defines an entire offence category as unattended theft, which makes its incidence count a direct measure.

Each is a partial view. They agree, which is the strongest thing that can be said in the absence of a direct count.

1. The measurement problem

Start with the obstacle, because it shapes everything after it. Crime statistics are built around legal categories — what offence was committed — not around situational circumstances, which is what 'opportunistic' describes. A theft from an unlocked car and a theft from a car whose window was smashed are both 'theft from a motor vehicle' in the US national count.

Our research covered the statistical agencies of five countries. The finding is consistent and worth stating as a result in its own right:

What this report does and does not claim

It does not claim to have found a hidden official statistic for opportunistic theft. It claims that four independent partial measures, in two countries, converge on the same conclusion, and it shows the arithmetic so the conclusion can be argued with. Every derived figure is computed in research/data_opportunistic.py from published inputs.

2. Question 1: what share of all theft is opportunistic?

Step one: classify the offence categories by guardianship

US larceny-theft breaks into nine published subcategories. Each can be sorted by where the property was and who was watching it. This classification is the report's one analytical judgement, so it is set out explicitly rather than buried:

The single cleanest fact in this report

Pocket-picking is 0.6% of US larceny and purse-snatching is 0.4%. Therefore 99.0% of US larceny-theft is property that was not in physical contact with its owner when it was taken. That figure requires no modelling and no assumptions — it is one subtraction from the FBI's own published distribution.

The US victim survey says the same thing from a different direction. NCVS classifies household property crime into burglary, motor vehicle theft and 'other theft', where other theft is formally defined as 'unlawful taking of property or cash without personal contact with the victim'. That category alone is 81.3% of the 13,637,450 property victimisations estimated in 2023. Source: US Bureau of Justice Statistics (NCVS), Criminal Victimization, 2023 (2023) ✓.

Step two: burglary confirms it from the entry side

Larceny tells you whether the owner was holding the item. Burglary tells you something better: whether the offender had to break anything. The FBI's burglary definition explicitly states that 'to classify an offense as a burglary, the use of force to gain entry need not have occurred', and the count is published split three ways.

In 2019 that is 37.8% of 1,117,696 burglaries, or roughly 422,489 offences in which nothing was forced. The figure is stable year to year (36.7% in 2018), which is what you want from a measure you intend to lean on.

Step three: the bounded estimate

Combining the two gives a range. The bounds differ in how the residual 'all other larceny' category (29.9% of larceny, containing everything not separately named) and motor vehicle theft are treated.

3. The UK measures it properly, and agrees

England and Wales is the only jurisdiction found that asks victims the questions that matter. Two kinds of evidence come out of it.

A whole offence category defined as unattended theft

The Home Office defines one of its theft categories in exactly the terms this report cares about: “Other theft of personal property covers thefts of unattended property where no force is used”. That makes its incidence count a direct measure. Setting it against 'theft from the person' — which is the contact category, covering both stealth and snatch — gives a clean ratio, and one that has moved sharply.

Expressed as a share, unattended theft was a remarkably stable 61–63% of personal-property theft from 2016/17 through 2022/23 — and then fell to 49% by 2025/26. That fall is not because unattended theft became rare; it is because snatch theft surged. Both channels matter, but the trendline is a warning against treating any single year as the answer.

Direct lock-status measures

The CSEW also asks, for vehicles and bicycles, whether the thing was actually locked. These are the most direct measurements of opportunistic theft that exist anywhere.

Over half of thefts from vehicles involve an unlocked door

52.8% of thefts from a vehicle in England and Wales involved a door that was simply not locked, and 45.4% of stolen bicycles were not locked at all. These are not thieves defeating security. They are thieves finding an absence of security.

The reasons victims gave for leaving a bike unlocked are the most useful lines in the whole dataset for anyone designing an intervention:

“Never thought about it” (29.6%) and “thought the area was safe” (23.9%) together account for over half. Neither is solved by a better lock. Both are attention and risk-perception failures, which is a different design target entirely — and a much cheaper one.

The older British Crime Survey went further and asked victims whether they felt partly responsible. In 26% of unattended-property thefts (base 888 incidents) victims said they were. Among those who did, the lapses named were: failed to lock property away 48%, left it open or visible 43%, failed to lock or bolt a door or window 15%, left a door or window open 2% (base 261). Those percentages describe only victims who already accepted some responsibility, and must not be read as shares of all incidents.

Source: UK Home Office / British Crime Survey, Crime in England and Wales 2008/09, Volume 1 (HOSB 11/09) (2009) ✓.

4. Where and when it happens

NIBRS records the location type of each offence. It does not record guardianship, so this is context rather than proof — but the shape is informative.

Residences (32.6%) and parking facilities (11.6%) together are nearly half of all recorded larceny. Both are places where people routinely leave property and walk away from it. Retail locations — department stores, supermarkets, convenience and specialty stores — account for another quarter, which is the shoplifting channel showing up in the location data.

The UK survey agrees: 62.3% of unattended-property thefts happened in daylight and 74.9% on a weekday. The most common single location was inside a workplace (16.6%). Opportunistic theft is a daytime, working-hours phenomenon that happens in the places people spend their ordinary lives.

Source: UK Office for National Statistics, Nature of crime: personal and other theft, Tables 1b & 2b (2024/25). Tables 1b and 2b; base 210 incidents.

5. Question 2: the campus picture

On campus the unattended share is higher than nationally, and the reason is structural. A campus has almost no retail theft and comparatively little theft-from-vehicle. What it has instead is a dense population of people carrying expensive, portable electronics between buildings, and putting them down constantly.

Answering Q2 directly

Campus sources do not support a single authoritative percentage, and this report will not invent one. What they support is this: on a university campus, somewhere between two-thirds and over 90% of theft is theft of property that was left unattended or unsecured. The lower bound comes from the one study with a clean denominator over all incidents of a theft type (UW-Madison, 69% of textbook thefts). The upper comes from a police force describing its whole reviewed caseload (Boston University, 90–95%).

Every additional institution examined — Miami, UT Austin, Kansas State, Caltech, Georgia Tech, Queen Mary — describes the same dominance in the same terms, without exception. Eight out of eight is not proof of a precise number, but it is strong evidence about the shape of the problem.

The campus burglary analogue

Queen Mary University of London's police analysis is the closest campus equivalent to the FBI's no-force burglary measure, and the result is more extreme than the national figure.

The intervention that followed is instructive: after a targeted campaign, recorded residential burglaries on campus fell from 12 to 0, bike thefts from 7 to 0, and total offences from 45 to 16 over comparable September–December windows (a 64% reduction).

What gets left unattended, and what happens when it does

Two campus field experiments, both Dutch, measure the two halves of the mechanism: how often students expose property, and whether anyone protects it when they do.

Zero out of fifty-four

In the Twente experiment a researcher openly removed a laptop from a library desk after the owner left. Where the owner had not asked anyone to keep an eye on it, not one of 54 bystanders intervened. Where the owner had asked, 84% did.

Four thefts were staged in sequence one to two metres apart; none of the guardians noticed the previous ones. And a Kansas State surveillance recording captured a laptop being unplugged and removed in 6 seconds.

The implication is uncomfortable and important: a crowded library provides essentially no passive protection. Presence is not guardianship. The systematic review of the guardianship literature reports spontaneous intervention rates in staged-crime studies ranging 0–40%, and notes that high-quality field tests are lacking. Source: van Dijk, University of Twente, Stolen in Plain Sight: Laptop Larceny in Academic Libraries (2025); Kansas State University IT News, Increased laptop thefts on campus are wake-up call (2010); Hollis-Peel, Reynald, van Bavel, Elffers & Welsh, Guardianship for crime prevention: a critical review (2011).

6. What actually gets taken

The obvious guess is “phones”. For opportunistic theft the obvious guess is wrong, and the way in which it is wrong is the most useful thing in this section. The item profile of unattended theft is almost the mirror image of the item profile of theft taken off a person.

The same survey, the same country, two completely different answers

Because the UK separates unattended theft from contact theft as distinct offence categories, and publishes an items-stolen table for each, the two can be set side by side directly. This is the cleanest available answer to the question.

Phones are a contact-theft problem, not an unattended-theft problem

Mobile phones are taken in 48.2% of thefts from the person and only 9.9% of unattended thefts — a five-fold difference in the same survey, in the same years, in the same country. In the latest single year the gap is wider still: 57.6% versus 5.4%.

The reason is obvious once stated: a phone is almost never the thing you put down and walk away from. It is in your hand or your pocket, which is precisely why it has to be snatched. Everything a person actually abandons for a few minutes — a coat, a bag, a laptop, a toolbox, a bike — is what turns up in the unattended column.

Any product aimed at opportunistic theft that is designed around protecting a phone is aimed at the wrong 10%.

There is no single ‘most stolen item’ in unattended theft

The second finding is the shape of the distribution rather than its top entry. Contact theft is concentrated: phones and wallets alone account for most of it. Unattended theft is flat. The largest named category, clothing, is under a fifth of incidents, and the residual ‘other’ category is the biggest single entry at 31.5% — which tells you the true distribution is even longer-tailed than the published one.

Note what sits high in the unattended column and is essentially absent from the contact column: tools or work materials (8.2% versus 0.1%) and food, toiletries and cigarettes (7.4% versus 0.3%). Nobody pickpockets a drill. These categories exist only because things get left in vans, workplaces, lockers and cloakrooms.

Value: same average, very different shape

The cost tables sharpen the picture in a way the item counts do not.

The five-fold median gap is the whole story

Mean loss is £487 for unattended theft and £499 for contact theft — statistically indistinguishable. But the median is £80 versus £400.

That means the two channels have completely different distributions hiding behind the same average. Contact theft is consistently expensive: the thief picked the target and picked well. Unattended theft is usually cheap with a fat tail: most incidents are a coat or a bag, but 17.3% exceed £1,000 and drag the mean up to meet contact theft.

Design consequence: an intervention for opportunistic theft has to be worth deploying for an £80 loss, because that is the typical case. It cannot be priced or designed around the laptop-sized tail.

The largest single unattended stream is a parked car

Switching from the UK to the US, and from items to offence volume, gives the other half of the answer. The FBI publishes larceny subtype counts with average values.

Theft from motor vehicles is the single largest named larceny subtype in the United States at 1,121,083 offences — more than shoplifting, and 29 times the combined total of pocket-picking and purse-snatching. If you want one sentence for what opportunistic theft looks like at national scale, it is somebody's belongings taken out of a parked car.

On campus the answer narrows sharply

Campus is the exception to the flat distribution, and this matters because campus is the deployment target. Two institutions publish item-level counts, and they agree.

On campus, the answer is bicycles and laptops

Boston University recorded 120 bicycle thefts and 61 computers, laptops or tablets against just 18 mobile phones in a single academic year. The University of Toronto, ranking item descriptions across 1,606 theft reports over six years, puts them in the order bicycles, money, laptops, bags, phones — phones last.

Both institutions independently reproduce the national pattern in sharper form. A campus concentrates exactly the two goods that are valuable, portable, and routinely parked: the bike you lock outside for three hours and the laptop you leave on a library desk while you get coffee.

The geography confirms the mechanism. Toronto's bike thefts cluster at the Athletic Centre (49 reports) and its laptop thefts at the Bahen Centre (20) and Robarts Library (19) — a gym and two study buildings. These are the places people must put their things down in order to do the thing they came for.

So what is the single most stolen thing?

Stated as precisely as the evidence allows:

  • By offence volume, nationally: property taken from a parked motor vehicle — 1,121,083 US offences in 2019, the largest named larceny subtype.
  • By item, in the tightest available unattended measure: no single item dominates. Clothing leads the named categories at 16.8% of incidents, ahead of cash (13.0%), phones (9.9%) and computers (9.5%).
  • On a university campus: bicycles first, laptops second, on the two institutions publishing counts.
  • What it is not: mobile phones. They dominate theft from the person and are a minor category in every unattended measure examined.

7. Where the stolen things go

Theft does not end when the item leaves. What happens next determines whether the theft was worth committing, and therefore whether it happens again. The disposal side turns out to be the softest point in the whole system — and the popular picture of it is wrong in two specific ways.

Disposal is not the aftermath. It is the motive.

Interviews with 50 prolific offenders in Shropshire found that ease of disposal was the most commonly reported reason for stealing a particular item — ahead of ease of theft and ahead of demand for the product. An Australian burglar study found the same. Disposability is the D in CRAVED, and offenders report it as the first consideration, not the last.

This is why the Activation Lock result in Section 3 worked. The UK Home Office put it plainly in its own review: Apple's more secure operating system 'seems to have dramatically altered the resale value of iPhones and also caused a marked fall in thefts'. Source: UK Home Office, Crime and the value of stolen goods (Research Report 81) (2015) ✓; ASU Center for Problem-Oriented Policing, Problem-Oriented Guides for Police: Stolen Goods Markets (2024) ✓.

Correction one: most stolen property is not sold at all any more

The best quantitative series on disposal is Australian. The Drug Use Monitoring in Australia programme ran a stolen-goods module across 7 waves between 2002 and 2017, interviewing 6,079 arrestees in police custody about what they usually did with what they stole. The trend is dramatic and runs opposite to the intuition.

By 2017, 64% of these offenders said they usually kept or used what they stole and only 11% usually sold it. Across the whole series, swapping for drugs averaged 13%, consuming the goods outright 9%, and 'spending them on other things' fell to 1% by 2017. The authors link the shift to the collapse in second-hand value of consumer electronics — there is simply less point selling a phone that will not activate or a television worth forty pounds.

Correction two: the buyer is a drug dealer, not a pawnbroker

When goods are sold, the stereotype of the shady pawnshop is almost the least likely destination.

The New South Wales prison interviews point the same way. Of 267 imprisoned burglars, around 70% admitted swapping stolen goods for illegal drugs, roughly half did so within an hour of the theft and over 90% within a day. About 80% said they had stolen goods to order.

Source: NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research, The stolen goods market in New South Wales (media release r44) (2001). Imprisoned-offender sample; not representative of all burglars, and self-reported.

Speed is the reason enforcement loses

Half of the NSW burglars converted goods to drugs within one hour, and over 90% within 24 hours. The Home Office's own offender interviews found stolen goods were usually sold within a day and were rarely dumped or given away.

Set that against the recovery funnel in Section 4. By the time a victim has noticed the loss, decided it is worth reporting, and been recorded, the item has typically already changed hands. Source: NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research, The stolen goods market in New South Wales (media release r44) (2001); Sutton, Schneider & Hetherington, UK Home Office, Tackling Theft with the Market Reduction Approach (Crime Reduction Research Series Paper 8) (2001).

What the thief actually gets

Stolen goods sell at a steep discount, which is the economic fact that makes high-volume low-value theft viable only in bulk.

Two consequences follow. First, an item has to be worth roughly three times the thief's effort threshold before it is worth taking for resale at all — which is exactly why so much opportunistic theft is now retention rather than resale. Second, in 2017 21 of 35 respondents who sold goods did not know what happened to them afterwards. The chain beyond the first handover is invisible even to the people in it.

Opportunistic theft specifically: much of it never enters a market

This is where the general picture and the opportunistic picture separate. The Home Office's review of stolen-goods value classifies which commonly stolen items would plausibly be resold at all — and several of the most frequently stolen are explicitly not resale goods.

Cash is the perfect stolen good, and it is the most stolen thing there is

Cash has been the single most frequently stolen item in every year the survey has run since 1981. It needs no fence, no buyer, no marketplace and no discount. It is not disposed of — it is the proceeds.

Around it sit the other non-market items: plastic cards, wallets and purses, documents and keys. The Home Office treats these as taken for their contents and then thrown away. Groceries, alcohol and cigarettes are consumed. None of these generate a resale trail, which means for a large share of opportunistic theft there is no market to disrupt and no second-hand listing to detect.

That is a hard constraint on any recovery product. The single most stolen thing in the world is untraceable by construction.

The six markets, for the goods that do get sold

The demand side is not marginal. Nationally representative British surveys found 11% of adults had been offered stolen goods in the previous year and 11% admitted buying some in the previous five; 70% thought at least some of their neighbours bought stolen goods for the home. A later self-report survey put buying at 7% of adults in a year and selling at 2.7%.

Modern channels: online, export, and the drug link

  • Online selling is growing from a small base. Australian arrestees reporting internet sale rose from 2% in 2012 to 9% in 2017. In a North American survey of 1,823 bicycle-theft victims, 15% recovered their bike, and 27% of those recovered were found being sold online — note the denominator is recovered bikes, not all stolen bikes.
  • Export defeats domestic blocking. The UK reports that 90% of handsets reported stolen are blocked across all networks within 48 hours — but blocked phones still work abroad. The Metropolitan Police charged a network that trafficked up to 40,000 stolen phones from the UK to China, which it believed accounted for about 40% of all phones stolen in London in the period. Street thieves were paid up to £300 per device; some sold for up to $5,000 in China. Over 10,000 iPhones were recovered.
  • Drugs are the engine. Around 45% of acquisitive crime is estimated to be committed by crack or opiate users, and 29% of arrested thieves in the UK are heroin or cocaine users. This is why immediacy beats price: the goods need to become drugs today, not fetch their best price next week.
  • Regulation of resale is being attempted. The US INFORM Consumers Act (in force June 2023) requires online marketplaces to collect and verify bank, identity and contact details for high-volume sellers, and to publish those details for the largest. No evaluation of its effect on stolen-goods resale has yet been published.

Source: Clare, Morgan, Lee & Voce, Drug Use Monitoring in Australia, Fencing markets and offender target selection (Psychology, Crime & Law) (2022) ✓; Findings (peer-reviewed), Patterns in bike theft and recovery (2023); UK Home Office, New code of practice to close multi-million pound stolen phones loophole (2010); Metropolitan Police, Three plead guilty following major mobile phone theft investigation (2026); UK Home Office, Crime and the value of stolen goods (Research Report 81) (2015) ✓; United States Congress, 15 U.S.C. 45f — INFORM Consumers Act (2023).

A widely quoted figure you should not use

The National Retail Federation's April 2023 report attributed nearly half of $94.5bn in retail shrink to organised retail crime. NRF withdrew the claim in December 2023 after the underlying source was shown to be a six-year-old inventory-loss survey that included non-theft causes. In NRF's own corrected figures, external theft is about 36% of shrink and organised retail crime is only a subset of that. Any pitch citing the retracted number is citing a withdrawn statistic. Source: Retail Dive, NRF updates crime report after faulty numbers (2023).

The strongest evidence in this report: close the channel, the theft stops

If disposal drives target selection, then removing a disposal channel should remove the theft. There is a clean natural experiment for exactly that. Between 2012 and 2013 the UK required scrap metal dealers to verify seller identity and banned cash payment, then introduced licensing.

Metal theft roughly halved. Scotland, which did not implement the measures over the same period, saw metal theft rise — which is consistent with displacement across the border and, awkwardly for the intervention, is exactly what you would expect if the mechanism is the market rather than the offender.

The pattern that keeps repeating

Three independent cases in this report share one mechanism. Apple made stolen iPhones unusable and iPhone robberies fell 38% in San Francisco. UW-Madison made stolen textbooks hard to sell and textbook theft fell 86%. The UK made stolen metal hard to sell for cash and metal theft roughly halved.

None of the three prevented a single theft attempt directly. All three removed the buyer. For property crime, the disposal channel is a more reliable intervention point than the offender, the victim or the object.

The caution for opportunistic theft specifically: the items most often taken opportunistically — cash, cards, wallets, food, clothing — largely have no channel to close. Channel-closing works brilliantly for phones, metal, textbooks and bicycles, and not at all for the single most stolen thing there is.

8. Why this changes what you should build

If opportunistic theft were a minority of theft, the rational response would be detection and enforcement. It is not a minority. It is the overwhelming majority, and its defining feature is that no security was defeated. That changes the target.

The one intervention the evidence most directly supports

The Twente result is the strongest actionable finding in this report, because it is a controlled campus experiment with an enormous effect size and a trivially cheap mechanism. Asking a stranger to watch your things moves bystander intervention from 0% to 84%.

Almost nobody does it, because asking is socially awkward and you have to pick someone, catch their eye and interrupt them. That awkwardness is the entire addressable problem, and it is a user-experience problem rather than a security one. A system that brokers the request — so the asker does not have to be socially brave and the watcher is explicitly, briefly accountable — is targeting the largest measured effect available, in the setting where the unattended share of theft is highest.

9. International: the measurement gap is global

Five national statistical systems were checked for circumstance data. The table below is what could be extracted. The absence of a single directly comparable international figure is a genuine result, not a research failure.

The ICVS is the nearest thing to an international circumstance measure: across 17 industrialised countries, only about one in three personal-property thefts involved the victim carrying the item at the time. The remaining two-thirds is consistent with this report's central finding, though the residual includes contexts beyond unattended property and cannot be claimed as a clean measure.

10. Limitations

  • The central US estimate is a reconstruction, not a count. It depends on classifying larceny subcategories by guardianship, which is a judgement. The classification is published in full above and in data_opportunistic.py so that a reader who disagrees can recompute it.
  • The strict/broad gap is wide (40% to 73%) because the 'all other larceny' residual is 29.9% of larceny and the FBI does not decompose it. Narrowing that gap would require NIBRS microdata analysis beyond this report's scope.
  • US category data is from 2019. The UCR-to-NIBRS transition broke the comparable national series; 2019 is the last clean year for the full subcategory distribution and for the burglary entry split.
  • UK nature-of-crime bases are small. The lock-status figures rest on unweighted bases of 195–475 incidents. They are indicative magnitudes, not precise values, and single-year movements should not be over-read.
  • The current ONS workbooks no longer ask directly about unattendedness. The explicit 'was the property left unattended' framing survives only in the offence-category definition and in the older 2008/09 British Crime Survey questions.
  • Campus figures have incompatible denominators. Boston University's 90–95% is of reviewed theft reports; UW-Madison's 69% is of textbook thefts; Miami's 'over half' is of all campus crime. They are not averageable and no average is offered.
  • Several campus sources are police estimates or prevention messaging, not audited counts. They are labelled as such in the table and should carry less weight than the two with real denominators.
  • The campus field experiments measure exposure and intervention, not theft. Twente staged thefts; nothing was actually stolen. A 0% intervention rate is not a 100% theft rate — it is an absence of guardianship, which is a necessary but not sufficient condition for theft.
  • Both field experiments are Dutch university libraries. Generalisation to other countries and campus cultures is untested.
  • Reporting bias runs underneath everything. Unattended-property theft is the least-reported theft type, so if anything these figures understate its share.
  • The item percentages are shares of incidents, not counts of objects. An incident can involve several item types, so the columns sum above 100% and no 'number of phones stolen' can be derived from them.
  • The 'other' category is the largest single entry in unattended theft (31.5%), so the true item distribution is longer-tailed than the published one and the clothing ranking is less secure than it looks.
  • Item bases are small. Even pooled over three years the unattended items table rests on 539 unweighted incidents; single years are 164–204. Rankings below the top three or four should not be treated as ordered.
  • Campus item counts come from two institutions. Boston University and Toronto agree, but two campuses in two countries is a pattern, not a population estimate, and the Toronto figures count report entries rather than incidents.
  • No US item-level distribution for unattended theft exists. FBI Table 23 gives larceny subtypes, not items; the NIBRS 2019 static tables contain no property-description-by-offence table, and a live Crime Data Explorer query for a property-stolen variable returned demographic dimensions instead. This gap was confirmed rather than assumed.
